Prep brief · Himalaya Robotics Hack

The shipped G1 policy has never seen ice.

MuJoCo Playground randomizes G1 ground friction over μ ∈ [0.4, 1.0]. Real ice sits between 0.05 and 0.15. There is no overlap. That gap is a two-day project, a 30-second demo, and the most defensible answer to "extreme-condition relevance" on the board.

Ground truth

Six things I verified that change the plan

Friction randomization 0.4 – 1.0 The full range in g1/randomize.py. Alongside mass ×[0.9,1.1], armature ×[1.0,1.05], qpos0 ±0.05. Nothing else.
G1 envs that exist 2 G1JoystickFlatTerrain and G1JoystickRoughTerrain. That's it.
G1 getup env None Go1Getup and SpotGetup ship; no humanoid equivalent. The recipe is portable — the convergence is not guaranteed.
Wind, in one line 55.6 m/s 200 km/h. MuJoCo subtracts option/@wind from body velocity in its fluid model. Set density and viscosity positive and you have drag.

The reading

What the three tracks actually cost

Track 1 — Movement

This is the track with the shortest path from zero to a working artifact, because Playground's G1 environment already runs. The joystick task is 29-dim position control at 50 Hz, ~103-dim observation (velocity, gyro, gravity, command, joint state, last action, gait phase), 1000-step episodes. Critically, the reward function already has the terms you need — feet_slip at −0.25, orientation at −2.0, feet_air_time at +2.0. Nobody tuned them for ice because nobody trained on ice.

So the work is not "build an ice environment from scratch." It is: widen the friction range downward, make friction heterogeneous across the floor, reweight three existing rewards, retrain. That is a tractable Saturday.

Track 2 — Action

Five bi-manual setups shared across every team is the binding constraint, not the ML. The LeRobot loop is well-trodden — teleoperate, record ~50 episodes, train ACT, run lerobot-rollout — but the docs say training "should take several hours" and recommend 50 episodes with fixed cameras and consistent grasps. Budget half a day of hardware queue before you have data.

The one idea here I'd fight for is rope-pull detection: the robot feels tension through joint torques and grips harder. It's genuinely mountaineering-specific (short-roping, belay), and it's a force-sensing problem more than a vision problem, which is unusual on a demo floor full of pick-and-place.

Track 3 — Thinking

Cheapest to build, easiest to under-score on technicality. The saving move is to attach it to something physical rather than shipping it standalone. Two numbers worth designing against: Iridium SBD carries 340 bytes up, 270 down, 5–20 s latency, billed per 50-byte block. A telemetry format that fits a full robot health report into one 340-byte burst is a real engineering constraint and reads as such to judges.

Recommendation

One sentence a judge repeats to another judge

The pitch

"The robot works out how slippery the ground is, changes how it walks, and tells basecamp — in 340 bytes."

Three layers over one shared quantity: the estimated friction under the feet. It touches three of the four things the organizers said they're building for, and each layer stands up on its own if the one below it fails.

EstimateTrack 3 · intelligence

Privileged critic sees true μ; the actor infers it from foot-velocity-during-stance and gyro residuals. Output is a scalar traction estimate at 50 Hz.

Falls back to: a supervised μ-regressor on logged rollouts. Trains in minutes, always demos.

AdaptTrack 1 · movement

Policy trained across μ ∈ [0.03, 1.0] log-uniform with heterogeneous patches. Expect it to discover shorter strides, lower CoM, longer double-support — the same things humans do on ice.

Falls back to: the μ estimate gating a hand-tuned conservative gait. Less impressive, still works.

ReportTrack 3 · comms

Pack traction, gait mode, slip events, battery and a go/hold/descend call into a single 340-byte Iridium SBD burst. Show the byte budget on screen.

Falls back to: nothing. This layer is pure encoding — it cannot fail, and it is cheap.

Demo is 20 points — protect it

Record the money shot early and don't depend on live training. The clip is stock policy versus yours, side by side, on μ = 0.08. Stock G1 splits and falls within a couple of steps — you can film that tonight, before you've trained anything, because it needs no policy work at all. Get the "before" in the can first.

Then, if and only if the spine converges by Saturday night, spend Sunday morning on the getup port. It's the highest-ceiling addition and the one most likely to eat the whole weekend if you start it first.

Compute

Nebius, and the one trap in it

Read this before you pip install anything

Nebius' GPU image family is ubuntu24.04-cuda13.0. Every MJX and Playground tutorial on the internet tells you to install jax[cuda12]. On this image you want jax[cuda13] — CUDA 13 wheels need driver ≥ 580, which the image has, and mixing the two is the classic way to lose an hour to a silent CPU fallback. Verify with jax.devices() before you trust a single timing number.

Which GPU

Take the H100, gpu-h100-sxm / 1gpu-16vcpu-200gb. At $3.85/GPU-hr a full 30-hour weekend is roughly $115, and G1 locomotion trains in ~15 minutes on that class of card — iteration speed is the entire game here. The L40S at $1.55/hr saves you about $70 and costs you roughly half your training runs. That is a bad trade on a two-day clock.

Three scripts, already written

  • nebius-up.sh — disk, subnet, cloud-init SSH key, instance, public IP. Override PLATFORM and PRESET as env vars to switch cards.
  • bootstrap.sh — runs on the VM: uv, jax[cuda13], mujoco, playground, brax, EGL headless rendering, then a smoke test that loads G1JoystickFlatTerrain and prints its observation and action sizes.
  • nebius-down.sh — stop halts GPU billing and keeps the disk; delete removes both, with a confirmation prompt.

The one step I can't do for you is nebius profile create — it opens a browser for federated login and needs your hands on it.

Rendering is headless now

No display on the VM, so the MuJoCo viewer is out. Render offscreen with MUJOCO_GL=egl, write mp4s, and scp them down. Worth getting working early — your demo clips are going to come through this path, and discovering it's broken on Sunday afternoon would be its own kind of disaster.

Tonight

About two hours, all of it remote

  1. Authenticate and bring the box up. nebius profile create, then ./nebius-up.sh, then bootstrap.sh on the VM. It ends by loading the G1 env, so if it completes you know the whole chain works. This is the single highest-value hour of prep.
  2. Prove the GPU is real. jax.devices() must report a GPU, not CPU. Then run one short PPO job on G1JoystickFlatTerrain and note the wall-clock — that number is your budget for every experiment afterwards.
  3. Read three files. g1/joystick.py, g1/randomize.py, go1/getup.py. Roughly 600 lines total, and they contain every lever you'll pull.
  4. Get the "before" clip. Load the stock G1 scene, set floor friction to 0.08, walk it, render to mp4 over EGL, scp it down. Zero training required and it's the first ten seconds of your presentation.
  5. Write the 340-byte packet spec. On paper. It's a design exercise, not code, and it forces you to decide what the robot actually knows.

At kickoff

Ask before you commit

01

Is there a pretrained G1 walking policy provided, or does everyone train from scratch? This changes the baseline you compare against.

02

Is a Jetson Thor on site to deploy to? Note the G1 EDU ships Orin at 100 TOPS — Thor is 128 GB and ~2070 FP4 TFLOPS at 130 W, so "runs on Thor" is a different claim from "runs on the robot."

03

Exactly which bimanual hands, and how is access queued across teams? Decides whether Track 2 is even reachable.

04

Is the physical G1 available for sim-to-real, and can you book floor time? A real robot shuffling on a wet plastic sheet beats any simulator video.

05

What does "data you collected from your phone" mean — is there a provided collection app or format?
